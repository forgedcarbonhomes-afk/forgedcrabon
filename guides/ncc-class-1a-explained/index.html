Buyer's Guide · Certification

NCC Class 1A, explained —
not just the marketing line

Almost every granny flat and tiny home ad mentions "Class 1A". Here's what the term actually requires, who's allowed to certify it, and how to tell a real certification from a claim.

What the National Construction Code actually classifies

The National Construction Code (NCC) sorts every building in Australia into classes based on its use — apartments, offices, warehouses, and so on. Class 1A covers a single detached dwelling, or one dwelling in a row of attached dwellings such as townhouses, separated by fire-rated walls. It's the classification a standard suburban house is built to. When a granny flat or small home is genuinely Class 1A certified, it has been assessed and approved to that exact standard — not a lighter, "good enough for a small building" version of it.

What the assessment actually covers

Class 1A certification isn't a single checkbox — it's an assessment against several NCC performance requirements at once, including:

  • — Structural adequacy — the frame and footings are engineered for the site's wind, soil and (where relevant) cyclone loading
  • — Fire safety — smoke alarm placement and type under BCA 3.7.2, and appropriate separation where dwellings are attached
  • — Damp and weatherproofing — wet areas assessed against AS 3740, envelope detailing to prevent water ingress
  • — Health and amenity — ventilation, natural light, ceiling heights and room sizes meeting minimum livability standards
  • — Energy efficiency — insulation and glazing performance meeting the relevant energy rating provisions

Electrical and plumbing sit alongside this, assessed against their own standards — AS/NZS 3000 for electrical, AS/NZS 3500 for plumbing and drainage — and must be carried out by licensed trades regardless of the building's NCC class.

Who actually certifies it — and why that matters

A manufacturer or builder cannot certify their own building as Class 1A. An independent, licensed building certifier assesses the design and the completed construction against the NCC and issues the certification — the same role that signs off on a standard house build. That independence is the entire point: it's what turns "we built this to a high standard" from a claim into a verifiable fact.

FAQ

Common questions

What does NCC Class 1A mean?

NCC Class 1A is the classification under Australia's National Construction Code for a single detached dwelling or a row of attached dwellings such as townhouses — the same class as a standard house. A building certified to this class has been independently assessed against structural, fire-safety, health, amenity and energy-efficiency provisions.

Who decides if a building is NCC Class 1A?

An accredited building certifier assesses the design and construction against the NCC and issues the certification — not the manufacturer or builder themselves. That independence is what separates a genuine Class 1A certification from a marketing claim of being "house-standard" or "built to code".

How do I check if a builder's Class 1A claim is genuine?

Ask to see the actual certification documentation — the certifier's name, licence number, and the specific approval — not just a line in a brochure. A legitimate builder will supply this on handover, and you can verify a certifier's licence through the Queensland Building and Construction Commission (QBCC).

Does Class 1A certification cover electrical and plumbing too?

The NCC classification itself covers the building envelope and structure. Electrical work must separately comply with AS/NZS 3000 and plumbing with AS/NZS 3500, both installed and certified by licensed trades — a genuinely certified home will have all of these, not just the structural classification.
